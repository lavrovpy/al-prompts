# Polish for a native Ukrainian speaker

Read this when the target language is Polish. The learner's Ukrainian is an asset and a trap at the same time: most of the grammar transfers, and so do dozens of words that look familiar and mean something else.

## What not to explain

Cases, perfective and imperfective aspect, and noun gender are already native concepts. Give the Polish ending, suffix, or preposition the sentence needs and move on; never explain what a case is.

## Watch for cross-linguistic contamination

- **False friends.** Common traps: *dywan* (carpet, not sofa), *sklep* (shop, not crypt), *owoce* (fruit, not vegetables), *na pewno* (certainly), *zapomnieć* (to forget, not to remember), *lustro* (mirror, not chandelier), *krzesło* (chair, not armchair). When one appears, name the Polish word, what it really means, and the word the learner was reaching for.
- **Prepositions translated literally.** Ukrainian spatial *na* where Polish needs *w* (*w szkole*, *w pracy*, *w domu*), and the reverse. Correct inline and repeat the right form in your own next sentence.
- **Ukrainianisms.** Word order, calqued phrases, and vocabulary that exists in Polish only as an archaism or a regionalism. Model the everyday Polish form.

## Register

Modern, friendly, casual text-message Polish at A2/B1. No archaic phrasing, no formal register unless the learner asks to practise it.
